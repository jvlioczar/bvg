100% Vegan means the entire menu is plant-based — order anything, no questions needed. Vegans don't eat meat, fish, dairy, eggs, honey, gelatine, or anything else made from animals — including ingredients that hide in labels, like casein and whey. Everything else is on the menu.

And that's a lot: vegetables, legumes (beans, lentils, chickpeas), grains and seeds, nuts, fruit, mushrooms, plant milks, tofu, seitan, meat and cheese alternatives, pasta, chocolate, and ice cream — as long as they're free of animal ingredients. It's not monotonous and it's not expensive; it just takes a little willpower and creativity.

For each vegan place we collect public reviews from Google, Facebook, HappyCow, Uber Eats, DoorDash, Tripadvisor, Yelp and OpenTable. The stars and review counts you see are combined from all of them.

The final rating (shown on a scale of 0 to 10) is not a simple average. It blends two statistical methods, a Bayesian Prior and the Wilson Lower Bound, and it gives more weight to platforms with more reviews, so places with only a few reviews are not ranked above places with thousands. Each venue page links to every source so you can check for yourself.

In one sentence: a strong rating needs both a high score and enough reviews standing behind it. Want the details? Here is how it works, step by step.

1. Bayesian Prior: the safety belt

A venue with only a few reviews can easily land an unusually high or low average, because a handful of experiences weigh a lot. To reduce that, the observed rating is gently pulled towards 3.5/5, our "we're not sure yet" point. The fewer the reviews, the stronger the pull; as reviews accumulate, the pull fades and the real rating takes over.

The Prior is calculated separately for each platform, and it is combined with Wilson only once, so the same uncertainty is never applied twice.

2. Wilson Lower Bound: the confidence floor

Wilson gives a more conservative estimate when the sample is small. We adapted it to a 1 to 5 scale and calculate it directly from the observed rating, not from the rating after the Prior, which is what keeps the Prior from being counted twice.

Each platform's score is 30% Bayesian Prior + 70% Wilson Lower Bound. Wilson gets the larger share so that a strong rating with plenty of reviews keeps most of its value, while small samples stay protected.

3. Review Confidence: how much a platform counts

Next we decide how much influence each platform should have, using Review Confidence. It has diminishing returns: going from 1,000 to 2,000 reviews matters a lot, going from 4,000 to 5,000 barely matters, because the evidence is already huge. Our confidence curve reaches approximately:

7.7% at 100 reviews · 34.0% at 500 · 58.4% at 1,000 · 86.2% at 2,000 · 96.5% at 3,000 · 98.8% at 4,000 · 99% at 5,000 (the maximum, more reviews do not raise it further).

This is not a claim that a rating is 99% likely to be right. It is a transparency and weighting mechanism showing how strongly the review volume supports a platform's result. Hover over (or tap) any rating on the site to see its confidence percentage.

4. Combining the platforms

Google, Facebook, HappyCow, Uber Eats, DoorDash, Tripadvisor, Yelp and OpenTable have very different audiences, volumes and habits, so each is processed independently first. Then the platform scores are averaged using their Review Confidence as weights. A platform with a handful of reviews has little influence, one with thousands has a lot, and the biggest platform can never drown out all the others just because of its raw count.

5. The published rating

The combined result is converted from the internal 0 to 5 scale to the published 0 to 10 scale, and that is the number you see on the site (the stars are just this number shown out of 5). The published rating has a minimum of 8.0/10. That is a floor on the published scale, not the statistical Prior: inside the model, results can still go below 4.0/5, and 3.5/5 remains the uncertainty point.

Behind the scenes, venues are ranked (for example under "Best rated") using the same result before it is rounded to one decimal, so two places that both show 9.0 are still put in the right order.

6. Why this is fair

A 4.8/5 backed by 4,000 reviews keeps most of its value, while a 4.8/5 from a few early reviewers is treated cautiously: the Prior pulls it down, Wilson trims it, and its platform gets little weight. These protections work together, and as reviews grow the venue gradually escapes them.

No single number tells the whole story. The model combines the quality of the rating, the quantity of evidence, statistical uncertainty and consistency across platforms, so that a strong rating is supported not only by how high the score is, but also by how much evidence stands behind it.
